## 1. What are the official guidelines, and what evidence sits behind them?

### Takeaway
Guidelines for under-5s still converge on "no screens (except video chat) under ~2, about 1 hour/day at ages 2–5, co-view, protect sleep and meals." This holds for WHO, the new UK 2026 guidance, Australia and China. For older children, the AAP's January 2026 policy dropped numeric caps and now focuses on what screens displace and on platform design. China's guidance is driven mainly by **myopia**: 15 min per session and ≤1 h/day of non-study screen use, ≤30% of teaching time on devices, ≥2 h/day of physical activity, and phones kept out of classrooms. The time thresholds are precautionary consensus values, not dose–response cut-offs derived from trials.

### Cited Findings

**International**
- **WHO (2019)**, *Guidelines on physical activity, sedentary behaviour and sleep for children under 5* [K]: <1 yr no screen time; 1 yr no sedentary screen time; 2 yrs ≤1 h ("less is better"); 3–4 yrs ≤1 h. Also sets sleep minimums (e.g., 3–4 yrs 10–13 h) and ≥180 min/day of physical activity for 1–4 yrs. WHO rated the evidence certainty as mostly low/very low. — [WHO](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241550536)
- **AAP (2016)**, "Media and Young Minds" [K]: avoid digital media other than video chat before 18–24 months. Ages 2–5: ≤1 h/day of high-quality programming, co-viewed. Ages 6+: consistent limits and a Family Media Use Plan. — [Pediatrics 2016;138(5):e20162591](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2016-2591)
- **AAP (Jan 20, 2026)**, policy statement "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents" plus a technical report, in *Pediatrics* [V]: **no fixed screen-time limit for older children**, replacing the earlier ~2 h framing. It shifts focus to what media replaces (sleep, play, outdoor time, in-person relationships), to engagement-maximizing platform design and commercial incentives, and to system-level and policy responses. It acknowledges benefits for learning, identity and connection when content is high quality and caregivers are involved. — [EdSurge](https://edsurge.com/news/2026-02-05-new-aap-screen-time-recommendations-focus-less-on-screens-more-on-family-time); [Contemporary Pediatrics](https://www.contemporarypediatrics.com/view/aap-calls-for-system-level-changes-to-improve-children-s-digital-media-environments); [AAP implementation page](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/understanding-the-new-AAP-digital-media-guidelines/)
  - **AAP family explainer and press release [V]:** infants under 18 months learn best from real-world interaction, and heavy solo screen use may affect language and social skills. The policy organizes evidence by age group (0–5, 6–12, 13–18). Recommendations to platforms include turning off chat features for minors. Technical report: *Pediatrics* 2026;157(2):e2025075321. — [HealthyChildren.org explainer](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/helping-kids-thrive-in-a-digital-world-aap-policy-explained.aspx); [AAP press release](https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/aap/2026/american-academy-of-pediatrics-calls-for-creating-a-child-friendly-digital-world/); [Technical report (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41556931/)
  - Caveat: the exact 2026 wording on video chat and any numeric limits for under-2s was not verified. **Gap.**
- **UK (27 Mar 2026)**, first national screen-time guidance for under-fives, published on the "Best Start in Life" site and based on the independent Early Years Screen Time Advisory Group (EYSTAG) report [V]: ages 2–5 "try to keep it to one hour a day. Less is possible". It advises avoiding "fast-paced social media-style videos and **AI toys or tools**" and keeping mealtimes and the hour before bed screen-free. It suggests "screen swaps" such as stories and games. Reports conflict on under-2s: some say "no screen time", others say "not alone" or "only shared/interactive". Check the primary text. EYSTAG says the advice should be reviewed as evidence becomes more certain. Guidance for **ages 5–16** was planned for **autumn 2026** after a call for evidence. Whether it was published by Nov 2026 was not verified. — [EYSTAG report (gov.uk)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69c53daf4a06660f085442a7/EYSTAG_report.pdf); [MadeForMums](https://www.madeformums.com/news/government-issues-first-screen-time-guidance-for-under-fives-including-one-hour-limit); [TheJournal.ie](https://www.thejournal.ie/under-fives-should-not-be-on-screens-for-more-than-an-hour-a-day-uk-parents-told-6996296-Mar2026); [MadeForMums on 5–16 guidance](https://www.madeformums.com/news/family-news/government-to-publish-screen-time-guidance-for-5-to-16-year-olds/)
  - Notable for this book: the UK is, as far as this research found, the first national government guidance to **explicitly name AI toys/tools** as something to avoid for under-5s. [V, same sources]
- **Australia** [K]: the 24-Hour Movement Guidelines set no screens under 2, ≤1 h at ages 2–5 and ≤2 h/day of recreational screen time at ages 5–17. The **Online Safety Amendment (Social Media Minimum Age) Act 2024** requires platforms to prevent under-16s from holding accounts, effective **10 Dec 2025**. — [Australian Dept of Health guidelines](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity-and-exercise/physical-activity-and-exercise-guidelines-for-all-australians); [eSafety Commissioner](https://www.esafety.gov.au/about-us/industry-regulation/social-media-age-restrictions). The age ban's effects have not yet been evaluated (see §3).
- **WHO (2020)** physical activity guidelines, ages 5–17 [K]: average ≥60 min/day of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) and limits on recreational screen time, with no numeric cap. — [WHO 2020 guidelines](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240015128)

**China**
- **2018 comprehensive myopia plan** (《综合防控儿童青少年近视实施方案》, eight ministries, Aug 2018) [K]: non-study electronic screen use ≤15 min per session and ≤1 h/day in total. Teaching use of electronic devices should in principle not exceed 30% of total teaching time, and homework should mainly be on paper. It also calls for daily outdoor activity. — [MOE](http://www.moe.gov.cn/srcsite/A17/moe_943/s3285/201808/t20180830_346672.html)
- **2024 notice on myopia in primary schools** [V]: teaching with electronic devices should in principle be ≤30% of total teaching time, and homework should in principle be on paper. — [bbtnews summary](https://www.bbtnews.com.cn/2026/1020/608502.shtml); [gmw.cn](https://m.gmw.cn/2026-10/21/content_1304577705.htm)
- **Oct 2026, 《关于防控儿童青少年高度近视的通知》**, issued by MOE, NHC and the National Disease Control Administration [V]: schools must reduce device use time in teaching, meet classroom lighting standards and provide adjustable desks and chairs. Students must get **≥2 h/day of comprehensive physical activity**, with "课间15分钟" (15-minute breaks between classes). It flags students whose myopia increases >50 degrees/yr or whose axial length grows >0.2 mm/yr. The reports found give **no new numeric screen cap**. — [Yicai](https://www.yicai.com/news/102354407.html); [gmw.cn](https://m.gmw.cn/2026-10/20/content_1304577335.htm); [cn-healthcare](https://www.cn-healthcare.com/articlewm/20261022/content-1679079.html)
- **Sept 2026 NDCPA press conference** [V]: a China CDC school-health expert repeated the advice of ≤15 min per non-study screen session and ≤1 h/day of after-school screen time in total. This is expert advice, not a binding rule. — [gmw.cn (Sept 2026)](https://m.gmw.cn/2026-09/07/content_38985351.htm)
- **NHC 近视防治指南 (2024 and 2026 editions)** [V]: the 2026 edition calls for eye-health checks from the newborn period and regular checks of visual acuity, refraction, **axial length** and corneal curvature from kindergarten, with closer follow-up for children with a family history of high myopia. — [People's Daily on the 2024 edition](https://paper.people.com.cn/hwbwap/html/2024-07/16/content_26069342.htm); [Xinhua 2026 (summary)](https://www.news.cn/politics/20260724/59ee7596a93f452c8763e5965dde46e9/c.html)
- **MOE Jan 2021 notice on phone management** (《教育部办公厅关于加强中小学生手机管理工作的通知》) [K]: primary and secondary students may not in principle bring phones into school. Exceptions need a written parental request, and phones must then be handed in and are banned from classrooms. Schools may not use phones to assign homework or require students to submit homework by phone. — [MOE](http://www.moe.gov.cn/srcsite/A06/s3321/202101/t20210126_511120.html)
- **Gaming and internet rules for minors** [K]: the NPPA notice of Aug 2021 limits minors' online gaming to 20:00–21:00 on Fri, Sat, Sun and statutory holidays. The 《未成年人网络保护条例》 (Regulations on the Protection of Minors Online) took effect 1 Jan 2024. The CAC's "minor mode" (未成年人模式) guidance was released as a 2023 draft with age-tiered daily limits (e.g., <8: 40 min; 8–16: 1 h; 16–18: 2 h). Whether those limits survived in the final version was **not verified**. — [NPPA notice via gov.cn](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2021-09/01/content_5634661.htm)

**Evidence behind the guidelines**
- **Myopia and outdoor time (causal)** [K]: in a cluster RCT in Guangzhou (He et al., *JAMA* 2015; 12 schools, n≈1,900 first-graders), one extra 40-min outdoor class per day cut 3-year myopia incidence from 39.5% to 30.4%. — [JAMA 2015;314(11):1142](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2015.10803)
- **Myopia and screens (correlational)** [K]: a meta-analysis by Foreman et al. (*Lancet Digital Health* 2021; 33 studies) linked smart-device screen time to myopia, with OR ≈1.26 for smart devices alone and ≈1.77 for smart devices plus computers. Heterogeneity was high and the studies were observational. — [Lancet Digit Health 2021;3:e806](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2589-7500(21)00135-7)
- **Sleep** [K]: a meta-analysis by Carter et al. (*JAMA Pediatrics* 2016; 20 studies, n≈125,000) found that bedtime access to media devices was associated with inadequate sleep quantity (OR ≈2.17), poor sleep quality (OR ≈1.46) and daytime sleepiness (OR ≈2.72). This is among the most consistent findings in the field, though the studies are largely observational. — [JAMA Pediatr 2016;170(12):1202](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341)
- **Developmental screening** [K]: in a longitudinal cohort by Madigan et al. (*JAMA Pediatrics* 2019; n=2,441, Calgary), more screen time at 24 and 36 months predicted poorer developmental-screening scores at 36 and 60 months, and the reverse direction was not supported. Effects were small and screen time was parent-reported. — [JAMA Pediatr 2019;173(3):244](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2018.5056)
- **Language** [V]: a meta-analysis by Madigan et al. (*JAMA Pediatrics* 2020; 42 studies, ~19,000 children <12) found that more screen time was associated with poorer language (r = −0.14, 38 studies), as was background TV (r = −0.19, 5 studies). Educational programming (r = 0.17, 4 studies) and co-viewing (r ≈ 0.16) were associated with **better** language. All studies were correlational. — [News-Medical summary](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20200323/Screen-time-impairs-childrens-language-skills.aspx); [JAMA Pediatr 2020;174(7):665](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2020.0327)

### Inferences
- **Age-band synthesis of guideline consensus:**
  - **0–3:** no solo screen media under ~18–24 months, except video chat with family, which has evidence of contingent learning (§2). From 2 yrs, ≤1 h of high-quality, co-viewed content. The UK adds no AI toys/tools.
  - **3–6:** ≤1 h/day, co-viewed, slow-paced, educational content. Screen-free meals and hour before bed. Lots of outdoor play (WHO ≥180 min of activity per day; China's myopia logic favours ≥2 h outdoors).
  - **6–12:** the global trend (AAP 2026) is away from numeric caps and toward displacement (sleep, MVPA ≥60 min, outdoor time, homework). China keeps an explicit **≤15 min per session, ≤1 h/day of non-study screen use** norm for myopia plus 2 h/day of physical activity, so Chinese parents face a stricter official number than US parents.
  - **12–18:** the policy focus shifts to social media design, age gates (Australia under-16), school phone rules (China 2021, England, the Netherlands and others) and sleep.
- Specific numbers (1 h, 2 h, 15 min) are expert consensus built on observational associations and precaution, not thresholds identified in trials. The strongest **causal** pieces are outdoor time → less myopia (RCT) and bedtime devices → worse sleep (consistent observational data plus plausible mechanisms).
- For a Chinese audience, myopia is the most concrete, measurable and causally supported reason to limit near-work and screen time for 3–12-year-olds. Outdoor light, more than "screens are poison", is the evidence-based lever.

### Gaps
- Full text of the AAP 2026 statement: whether the under-2 and 2–5 numbers were retained was not verified.
- Final official wording of the UK guidance for under-2s; whether the UK 5–16 guidance was published by autumn 2026.
- Final CAC "minor mode" time tiers (draft vs final).
- Australia's under-16 ban: early process data exist (see §3), but there are no mental-health outcomes yet.
- Latest official Chinese child and adolescent myopia prevalence figures (NHC reported ~52–53% around 2018–2022 [K, unverified]).

## 2. Video deficit, serve-and-return interaction, language development and "technoference"

### Takeaway
It is well established that infants and toddlers learn far less from video than from live, responsive people. The meta-analytic "video deficit" is about 0.5 SD and shrinks with age. Contingent, back-and-forth talk (serve-and-return / conversational turns) predicts language and brain development better than the quantity of words heard. Video chat that is responsive narrows the deficit. Parental phone use ("technoference") is consistently associated with worse parent–child interaction and child behaviour, but the evidence is mainly correlational and bidirectional.

### Cited Findings
- **Video deficit meta-analysis** [V]: Strouse & Samson (*Child Development* 2021; 59 reports, 122 effect sizes, ages 0–6) found an average deficit of ~**0.5 SD** for learning from screens vs live demonstration. It shrinks with age, is larger for object-retrieval tasks, and is no different for live vs prerecorded video. — [Strouse & Samson, Child Dev 2021, 92(1):e20](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13429); summary of the effect size in [Kirkorian et al. meta-analysis PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Heather-Kirkorian-2/publication/369969171_Screen_media_exposure_and_young_children's_vocabulary_learning_and_development_A_meta-analysis/links/643aee852eca706c8b5f9e95/Screen-media-exposure-and-young-childrens-vocabulary-learning-and-development-A-meta-analysis.pdf)
- **Social gating of language** [K]: in Kuhl, Tsao & Liu (*PNAS* 2003), 9-month-old American infants exposed to Mandarin through a **live** speaker (12 sessions) kept the ability to discriminate Mandarin speech sounds. Infants who got the same input by **video or audio** showed no learning. Samples were small (n≈16 per group) but the finding is foundational. — [PNAS 2003;100(15):9096](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1532872100)
- **Contingency rescues screen learning** [K]: in Roseberry, Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff (*Child Development* 2014), 24–30-month-olds learned novel verbs from socially contingent video chat but not from non-contingent video. Myers et al. (*Developmental Science* 2017) found that 17–25-month-olds recognised and learned from a FaceTime partner but not from a prerecorded video. — [Child Dev 2014;85(3):956](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12166); [Dev Sci 2017;20(4):e12430](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.12430)
- **Conversational turns and the brain** [K]: in Romeo et al. (*Psychological Science* 2018; n=36, ages 4–6), the number of adult–child conversational turns, more than adult words, predicted Broca's-area activation during language processing and mediated SES differences in verbal skill. The study was correlational and small. — [Psychol Sci 2018;29(5):700](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617742725)
- **Long-run prediction** [K]: in Gilkerson et al. (*Pediatrics* 2018; n=146, LENA recordings), conversational turns at 18–24 months predicted IQ and vocabulary about 10 years later. The study was correlational. — [Pediatrics 2018;142(4):e20174276](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2017-4276)
- **Serve-and-return framework** [K]: Harvard's Center on the Developing Child describes responsive back-and-forth interaction as a core mechanism of early brain architecture. This is a framing synthesis, not a single study. — [Harvard CDC](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/science/key-concepts/serve-and-return/)
- **Contested: the "30-million-word gap"** [K]: Hart & Risley's (1995) word-gap estimate rests on 42 families. A replication-style study (Sperry, Sperry & Miller, *Child Development* 2019) found that counts vary widely once bystander talk is included. The **turn-taking** findings are more robust than "word count" claims. — [Child Dev 2019;90(4):1303](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13072)
- **Screen time and language (correlational)** [V]: in Madigan et al. (2020), more screen time and background TV were associated with poorer language, while educational content and co-viewing were associated with better language (see §1). — [JAMA Pediatr 2020](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2020.0327)
- **Background TV** [K]: background TV reduces both the quantity and quality of parent talk to toddlers (Kirkorian et al., *Child Development* 2009, lab study) and disrupts toy play (Schmidt et al., 2008). — [Child Dev 2009;80(5):1350](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2009.01337.x)
- **Technoference** [K]:
  - McDaniel & Radesky (*Child Development* 2018; n=170 couples with young children) found that parent-reported technology interference in parent–child interactions was associated with more child externalizing and internalizing behaviour. A follow-up (*Pediatric Research* 2018) found bidirectional effects: children's difficult behaviour also predicted more parental device withdrawal. — [Child Dev 2018;89(1):100](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12822); [Pediatr Res 2018;84:210](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41390-018-0052-6)
  - Myruski et al. (*Developmental Science* 2018; n≈50 infants 7–20 months) used a "still-face with phone" paradigm: when mothers looked at their phone, infants showed more negative affect and less exploration. This is a lab experiment. — [Dev Sci 2018;21(4):e12610](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.12610)
  - Reed, Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff (*Developmental Psychology* 2017; n=38 mothers of 2-year-olds) found that toddlers learned a novel word when teaching was uninterrupted but not when a mother's phone call interrupted it. This is an experiment, but small. — [Dev Psychol 2017;53(8):1428](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000292)
  - **Meta-analysis (JMIR 2025) [V]:** Zhang et al. linked parental technoference with child *problematic media use*: pooled r = 0.296 (95% CI 0.259–0.331), with individual studies ranging from r≈0.11 to 0.54. Most studies were cross-sectional. The authors propose observational learning, meaning children model their parents' device habits. — [J Med Internet Res 2025;27:e57636](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11799820)
  - **Systematic review and meta-analysis (JAMA Pediatrics 2025) [V]:** across 21 early-childhood studies (~14,900 participants), parents' technology use in the child's presence was associated with worse child cognitive and psychosocial outcomes and more child screen time. **Effect sizes were small** and the evidence is mainly correlational. — [JAMA Pediatrics abstract](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/article-abstract/2833506)
- **Executive function and pacing** [K]: Lillard & Peterson (*Pediatrics* 2011; n=60 4-year-olds) found that 9 min of a fast-paced cartoon immediately reduced executive-function task performance vs an educational cartoon or drawing. The experiment was small, effects were short-term, and later replications were mixed. — [Pediatrics 2011;128(4):644](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2010-1919)
- **Brain imaging** [K]: Hutton et al. (*JAMA Pediatrics* 2020; n=47 aged 3–5) linked screen use above AAP guidance to lower white-matter integrity. The study was cross-sectional and small, and media coverage frequently overstated it. — [JAMA Pediatr 2020;174(1):e193869](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2019.3869)

### Inferences
- **0–3:** the most robust message is that the person, not the device, is the teacher. Live, contingent talk and play are irreplaceable. Video chat with grandparents is the clearest exception, because it is contingent. This maps directly onto the UK 2026 "screen swaps" and avoid-AI-toys advice.
- **3–6:** the video deficit narrows. Well-designed, slow-paced educational programming plus co-viewing (talking about what is on screen) is associated with neutral-to-positive language outcomes, while background TV and fast-paced content are the riskier patterns.
- **For AI-anxious parents:** the serve-and-return literature implies that the biggest screen risk for toddlers may be the **parent's** phone (technoference), not the child's. This is actionable and less fear-inducing, but the causal evidence is weaker than the correlational evidence.
- **Open question:** conversational AI is contingent but not socially attuned. Whether it behaves like video chat (which helps) or like video (which shows a deficit) for toddlers' language is unknown (see §4).

### Gaps
- The exact pooled effect sizes of the 2025 JAMA Pediatrics technoference review were not extracted (abstract-level information only).
- No studies were found on toddlers' language learning from LLM voice agents. This appears to be a true research gap as of Nov 2026.
- Chinese-sample replications of the video deficit or technoference were not searched.

## 3. Smartphones and social media and adolescent mental health: the state of the debate, and the evidence on school phone bans

### Takeaway
The debate is unresolved. Haidt and Twenge argue that smartphones and social media are a major cause of the post-2012 rise in adolescent anxiety and depression, especially among girls. Odgers, Orben, Przybylski, Candice Odgers' collaborators and others argue that the associations are small (r ≈ 0.05–0.15), possibly bidirectional, and confounded, and that the causal case is unproven. Experimental and quasi-experimental studies (mostly on adults or college students) show small benefits from reducing social media. The best evidence on school phone bans (England, Feb 2025) found that restrictive policies cut in-school use but showed **no wellbeing or attainment benefit**. A few quasi-experimental studies elsewhere report academic gains.

### Cited Findings
**The debate**
- **Haidt's position** [V]: in *The Anxious Generation* (2024) and the World Happiness Report 2026 chapter with Zach Rausch, Haidt argues that "social media is not safe for adolescents", while conceding that population-level harm requires more extrapolation. — [The Register on WHR 2026](https://www.theregister.com/2026/03/19/social_media_bad_for_kids/); [After Babel](https://www.afterbabel.com/p/social-media-debate-longitudinal)
- **Odgers' critique** [V]: Odgers argues that the causal claims are premature, that teens with existing problems use platforms more or differently (reverse causation), and that family, socioeconomic and community factors matter. — [Scholars & Storytellers summary](https://scholarsandstorytellers.com/blog/haidt-odgers-debate); [Odgers, *Nature* 2024 review of The Anxious Generation](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-024-00902-2) [K]
- **Specification-curve study** [K]: Orben & Przybylski (*Nature Human Behaviour* 2019; ~355,000 adolescents in three UK/US datasets) found that digital technology use explained at most **0.4%** of the variance in wellbeing (median β ≈ −0.03), comparable to eating potatoes. Twenge, Haidt and others replied that heavy-use groups and social media specifically, especially for girls, show larger associations. — [NHB 2019;3:173](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1)
- **Orben's reviews** [V]: links were found in both directions, with negative associations at best "very small". — [Scholars & Storytellers](https://scholarsandstorytellers.com/blog/haidt-odgers-debate)
- **Bidirectionality** [V]: a longitudinal study found that depressive symptoms predicted small increases in social media use, but not the reverse. Haidt's team disputes the interpretation. — [After Babel](https://www.afterbabel.com/p/social-media-debate-longitudinal)
- **Developmental windows** [K]: Orben, Przybylski, Blakemore & Kievit (*Nature Communications* 2022; ~84,000 UK participants) found windows where higher social media use predicted lower life satisfaction a year later, around **11–13 for girls and 14–15 for boys**, and again at ~19. Effects were small. — [Nat Commun 2022;13:1649](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-29296-3)
- **Quasi-causal (natural experiment)** [K]: Braghieri, Levy & Makarin (*American Economic Review* 2022) studied the staggered rollout of Facebook across US colleges, 2004–06. The rollout increased an index of poor mental health by ~0.085 SD, a sizeable share of it in depression. The study covers college students, not teens, and the Facebook of that era. — [AER 2022;112(11):3660](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20211218)
- **RCT (adults)** [K]: in Allcott et al. (*AER* 2020; n≈2,700 US adults), four weeks of Facebook deactivation improved subjective wellbeing by ~0.09 SD. — [AER 2020;110(3):629](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658)
- **Meta-analyses of reduction experiments are contested** [K]: some (e.g., Ferguson 2024, *Psychology of Popular Media*) report near-null pooled effects, while Haidt's team reanalyses the same trials as showing benefits. The exact effect sizes were not verified this session. **Flag as contested.**
- **RCT in youth aged 17–25 [V]:** a parallel RCT in *Psychology of Popular Media* (n=220 emotionally distressed 17–25-year-olds) asked the intervention group to cut smartphone social media use to 1 h/day for 3 weeks. Compared with unrestricted controls, it produced significantly greater reductions in depression, anxiety and FOMO and greater sleep gains. This is causal evidence, but the sample is older and at risk, the trial was short, and outcomes were self-reported. — [Bath research portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/social-media-detox-and-youth-mental-health/) (PubMed 41284297); [EdSource](https://edsource.org/updates/page/study-short-term-social-media-breaks-can-improve-mental-health-in-youth)
- **"One-week detox" (JAMA Network Open, late 2025) [V]:** widely covered as showing reduced depression, anxiety and insomnia in young adults, but it is an **observational cohort, not an RCT**. — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/12/02/nx-s1-5627156/social-media-digital-detox-depression-anxiety)
- **Adolescents under 17 [V]:** no large RCT with published results was found. A UK school-based cluster RCT (the "IRL Trial", primary outcome RCADS-25 anxiety) is registered, but no results were located. — [ISRCTN75400619](https://www.isrctn.com/pdf/ISRCTN75400619)

**Australia's under-16 social media minimum age (in force 10 Dec 2025): early evaluation [V]**
- eSafety's official evaluation is a longitudinal study of more than 4,000 children and families, reporting in stages through 2026–2028. The first (three-month) report found:
  - the share of under-16s **holding** an account fell from 52.4% to 42.1%, a modest but statistically significant drop;
  - the share **using** platforms fell only from 85.9% to 81.5%;
  - most under-16s who had accounts before the ban kept them or created new ones;
  - platforms revoked about 4.7 million accounts.
- There are no wellbeing outcome data yet. — [eSafety three-month report (PDF)](https://www.esafety.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-07/Early-days-early-insights-SMMA-evaluation-three-months-July-2026.pdf); [eSafety early insights](https://esafety.gov.au/newsroom/media-releases/early-insights-from-esafetys-comprehensive-evaluation-project); [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/3/australias-under-16-social-media-ban-failing-study-shows-what-it-means); [eSafety Nov 2026 update (PDF; not read in detail)](https://www.esafety.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-11/SMMA_Evaluation_Understanding_experiences_of_social_media_age_restrictions_update.pdf)

**School phone bans**
- **SMART Schools study** [V]: Goodyear et al. (*Lancet Regional Health – Europe*, Feb 2025) was a cross-sectional study of 30 English secondary schools (20 restrictive, 10 permissive) and **1,227 students aged 12–15**. Restrictive policies cut in-school phone use (median ~10 min vs ~1 h) but did **not** reduce total daily phone or social media time, and showed **no differences** in mental wellbeing, anxiety, depression, sleep, physical activity, attainment or classroom behaviour. However, **more phone and social media use overall was associated with worse outcomes** on all these measures. The authors call for a "holistic" approach and say bans alone are not enough. — [University of Birmingham](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2025/school-bans-alone-not-enough-to-tackle-negative-impacts-of-phone-and-social-media-use); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20250207/School-phone-bans-show-no-mental-health-or-academic-benefits.aspx); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/school-smartphone-bans-save-time-but-dont-improve-student-mental-health-study-finds/); [Research portal](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/school-phone-policies-and-their-association-with-mental-wellbeing/)
  - Follow-ups from the same programme [V] include a health-economics analysis (small savings in staff time spent on phone behaviour) and a 2026 qualitative paper in *Social Science & Medicine* ("not the silver bullet"). — [Univ. of Birmingham 2026](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2026/school-phone-bans-or-not-digital-lives-of-students-affect-the-school-day)
- **Quasi-experimental positive findings** [K]:
  - Beland & Murphy (*Labour Economics* 2016; English schools, staggered bans) found test scores up ~6.4% of an SD, concentrated among low achievers. — [Labour Econ 2016;41:61](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004)
  - Abrahamsson (Norway, working paper 2024) found that bans were associated with less bullying, fewer mental-health consultations and better grades, especially for girls from low-SES backgrounds. It is not peer reviewed as of last check. — [NHH working paper](https://openaccess.nhh.no/nhh-xmlui/handle/11250/3114581)
- **China** [K]: national policy since 2021 keeps phones out of classrooms (§1). No Chinese evaluation of its effects on mental health was found. **Gap.**

### Inferences
- **6–12:** the evidence supports delaying personal smartphones and social media and protecting sleep (no devices in the bedroom). The precautionary case is reasonable even though the causal evidence is not decisive. The adolescent "sensitive windows" (girls ~11–13) suggest that early adolescence is the period to be most careful.
- **12–18:** total use and displacement (sleep, exercise) appear to matter more than in-school bans. A school ban alone is unlikely to change wellbeing unless home use also changes. This fits China, where school bans already exist but home use is unregulated except for gaming.
- Effect sizes at the population level are small on average, but averages can hide large effects for vulnerable subgroups (heavy users, girls, kids with existing problems). Both sides of the debate broadly accept this point.
- For AI-anxious parents, the lesson from 15 years of the smartphone debate is that the science arrived later than the technology and remains contested. The same will likely apply to AI companions, which argues for precaution plus monitoring rather than certainty.

### Gaps
- No verified adolescent RCTs of social media reduction, and no exact pooled effect sizes from meta-analyses of experiments.
- Australia's ban has only three-month process data so far (see above). Mental-health outcome data are not expected until 2027–28.
- No Chinese longitudinal data on adolescent social media/短视频 use and mental health was searched.

## 4. Children and conversational AI and voice assistants: anthropomorphism, trust and learning from AI agents

### Takeaway
Children readily attribute minds to voice assistants and AI agents, though they rate them below humans, and this declines with age. They often trust them as information sources. Well-designed conversational agents can match human partners on story comprehension in short lab studies. Evidence is early, mostly short-term lab work with small samples. **Nothing is known about the long-term developmental effects of daily LLM companion use.**

### Cited Findings
- **Mind perception in generative AI** [V]: Xu, Thomas, Yu & Pan (*Computers in Human Behavior: Artificial Humans*, May 2025; n=119, ages 4–8) had children co-create stories with a generative AI agent via a speaker, a present human, or a hidden human, to study when children attribute minds to AI. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/0a514ac0ee80467e87bba42b610ca21f) (DOI 10.1016/j.chbah.2025.100135)
- **Learning from conversational agents** [K]: Xu et al. (*Child Development* 2022, also *Computers & Education*) studied 3–6-year-olds who answered an AI agent's questions while listening to a story. They showed comprehension gains **comparable to those with a human partner** and greater than with no-dialogue listening. Effects were short-term and samples were ~90–100. Xu's 2026 talk reportedly describes higher recall and transfer for an AI-conversation condition in a PBS Kids experiment [V, but from a talk summary, not a paper]. — [Child Dev 2022;93(2):e149](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13708); [Talk summary](https://www.pulse.bot/ai/video/ying-xu-ai-and-the-developing-child-myths-evidence-and-open-questions-c9027713-a2b2-4b05-a86d-b1729739b092/)
- **Attributions to voice assistants** [K]: Girouard-Hallam, Streble & Danovitch (*Human Behavior and Emerging Technologies* 2021; ages 6–10) found that children attributed some mental capacities (thinking, intelligence) to Alexa but few social or emotional ones, and attributions decreased with age. Andries & Robertson (2023, "Alexa doesn't have that many feelings") report similar nuanced views. — [Hum Behav Emerg Tech 2021;3(5):1118](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbe2.321)
- **Trust and reliance** [V]: in an arXiv study of an age-tailored "Ask Me Anything" chatbot for K–8 students (Vahedian Movahed & Martin, 2025; preprint), students showed **high trust and reliance** on its answers, with anthropomorphizing and relationship-building themes. — [arXiv 2502.14217](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2502.14217)
- **Learning about AI changes trust** [V]: Van Brummelen, Tian & Kelleher (AAAI) found that teaching children and parents about AI changed their partner models and trust in agents. — [AAAI](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/26883)
- **Selective trust literature** [K]: from about age 4, children prefer accurate over inaccurate informants (Koenig & Harris, 2005). Studies extending this to robots and devices find that children also track an agent's accuracy, which suggests that **teaching children to check AI accuracy builds on an existing capacity**. — [Child Dev 2005;76(6):1261](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2005.00849.x)
- **Teen use of AI companions (survey)** [K]: a Common Sense Media 2025 survey (n≈1,060 US teens 13–17) reported that ~72% had used AI companions and about half used them regularly, and roughly a third had discussed serious matters with an AI instead of a person. The figures were not re-verified in-session. — [Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/talk-trust-and-trade-offs-how-and-why-teens-use-ai-companions)
- **Policy signal** [V]: the UK March 2026 guidance tells parents of under-5s to avoid "AI toys or tools". — [MadeForMums](https://www.madeformums.com/news/government-issues-first-screen-time-guidance-for-under-fives-including-one-hour-limit)

### Inferences
- **3–6:** children's anthropomorphism is normal and mostly "mixed" (smart but not feeling). Short structured dialogic use (AI asks questions about a story) has the best evidence of benefit. Open-ended companion chat has none.
- **6–12:** this is the age when children can be taught that AI can be wrong. Selective-trust research suggests they can learn to check sources, which is a strong, hopeful message for parents.
- **12–18:** the main concern shifts from cognition to emotional reliance and companion apps. Survey data show heavy uptake, but outcome research is absent.
- AI's contingency (it responds) could make it a better teacher than passive video for young children, but its lack of genuine shared attention and emotional attunement makes it a poor substitute for serve-and-return with a caregiver. This is a hypothesis, not established.

### Gaps
- No longitudinal studies of developmental outcomes from regular LLM or AI-companion use by children.
- No Chinese-sample studies on children and AI agents (e.g., 小度, 豆包, AI learning machines) were found or searched.
- Evidence on AI toys specifically (behind the UK warning) was not located. The EYSTAG report should be checked for its basis.

## 5. Cognitive offloading and LLMs: "Your Brain on ChatGPT" and related evidence, and how robust it is

### Takeaway
Using LLMs to *produce* work reduces engagement and memory for that work, and unguided AI help on practice can lower later unaided performance. The best causal evidence is a ~1,000-student field experiment in PNAS 2025. However, the widely cited MIT "Your Brain on ChatGPT" EEG study is a **small (n=54), non–peer-reviewed preprint** with published methodological critiques. Well-designed AI tutors that withhold answers show neutral-to-positive learning effects. The design and use pattern matters more than the tool itself.

### Cited Findings
- **"Your Brain on ChatGPT"** [V]: Kosmyna et al. (MIT Media Lab, arXiv preprint, **June 2025**; n=54 split into LLM, search-engine and brain-only groups; essays across sessions, with only 18 completing session 4). The LLM group showed the weakest EEG connectivity and poorer recall of and ownership over their own essays. The authors call the conclusions preliminary. **Not peer reviewed.** Participants who started brain-only and then switched to the LLM retained higher engagement. — [arXiv 2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872v1); [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/)
  - **Critique** [V]: Stanković et al. (arXiv comment, Dec 2025/Jan 2026) cite the limited sample size, reproducibility, EEG-analysis issues, inconsistent reporting and limited transparency. — [arXiv 2601.00856](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00856v1)
  - **Author's clarifications** [V]: the study did not measure IQ and found no "brain rot", and the authors asked media to avoid "dumb" or "brain rot" language. EEG connectivity reflects task engagement, not lasting deficits. — [The Post PH](https://thepost.ph/money/technology/chatgpt-mit-study-brain-cognitive-test-2025/)
- **Field RCT, high school math** [V]: Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman (*PNAS* 2025, 122(26):e2422633122; ~1,000 students in Turkey) compared GPT-4 "Base" (ChatGPT-like) with "Tutor" (guardrails: hints, no direct answers). Both improved practice performance, but Base students scored **~17% lower on later unaided exams** than controls, with students using AI as a "crutch". Tutor largely eliminated the harm. Limitations: one country, one subject, short duration. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html); [Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486)
- **Positive AI-tutor RCTs** [K]:
  - Kestin et al. (*Scientific Reports* 2025; Harvard physics, n≈194, crossover) found that a pedagogically designed AI tutor produced larger learning gains than an in-class active-learning session. — [Sci Rep 2025;15:17458](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6)
  - A World Bank Nigeria pilot (De Simone et al., 2025, working paper) of six weeks of after-school GPT-4 tutoring with teacher guidance reported ~0.3 SD gains in English. — [World Bank blog](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/From-chalkboards-to-chatbots-Transforming-learning-in-Nigeria)
- **Correlational survey** [K]: Gerlich (*Societies* 2025; n=666 UK adults) found a strong negative correlation between AI-tool use and critical-thinking scores, mediated by cognitive offloading, and stronger in younger participants. It is a cross-sectional self-report study and cannot establish direction. — [Societies 2025;15(1):6](https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006)
- **Knowledge workers** [K]: Lee et al. (Microsoft Research/CMU, CHI 2025; n=319) found that higher confidence in GenAI was associated with less critical thinking, and higher self-confidence with more. The study was survey-based. — [CHI 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778)
- **Background theory** [K]: Risko & Gilbert (*Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 2016) describe offloading as a rational strategy that frees resources but reduces internal memory for the offloaded content. The famous "Google effect" (Sparrow et al., *Science* 2011) **failed to replicate** in the Camerer et al. 2018 large replication project. — [TiCS 2016;20(9):676](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002); [Camerer et al., NHB 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0399-z)

### Inferences
- **Robustness ranking:**
  1. Strongest: unguided AI → worse unaided performance on practiced skills. This rests on a field RCT, is consistent with offloading theory, and replicated "desirable difficulties" research.
  2. Moderate: guided or tutor-style AI → equal or better learning. There are several RCTs, but with short durations and specific designs.
  3. Weak: EEG "cognitive debt" and survey "AI erodes critical thinking". These are a preprint or correlational.
- **6–12:** the parenting principle that follows is "learn the skill first, then use AI." Mirroring the study participants who went brain-first, children should draft and attempt problems themselves before AI assistance, and AI should give hints rather than answers. In Chinese homework culture (AI 学习机, 拍照搜题 photo-search answer apps), "answer-giving" use maps onto the harmful Base condition.
- **12–18:** writing is thinking. Outsourcing essays reduces memory and ownership of the content (Kosmyna, directionally consistent with the RCT), so assign AI a role as critic or tutor rather than author.
- No study has measured long-term cognitive development in children who grew up with LLMs. All claims about "a generation losing the ability to think" are extrapolation.

### Gaps
- No RCTs found on primary-school-age children using LLMs for homework.
- No Chinese studies on 拍照搜题 or AI 学习机 and learning outcomes were searched (the search budget was exhausted).
- Peer-review status of Kosmyna et al. as of Nov 2026 is unknown. The last confirmed status is an arXiv preprint.
